How to Compute Take-Home Pay in the Philippines

Take-home pay is what you receive after SSS, PhilHealth, Pag-IBIG and withholding tax. Here are the steps, a worked example and a free calculator.

Take-home pay is what you actually receive after required deductions. To work it out, start with your gross pay. Then subtract your share of SSS, PhilHealth and Pag-IBIG contributions, and your withholding tax.

Net pay = Gross pay − SSS − PhilHealth − Pag-IBIG − Withholding tax

Below, I go through each step with an example. You can also try your own numbers in the Take-Home Pay Calculator. The results are estimates.

What Is Take-Home Pay?

Take-home pay, also called net pay, is the money left after deductions come out of your gross salary. Gross pay is what you earn before deductions. Net pay is what you receive.

Many Filipino workers call their salary sahod. The sahod in your job offer is usually higher than what reaches your bank account, because SSS, PhilHealth, Pag-IBIG and withholding tax are taken out first.

Some employers pay once a month. Others pay twice a month, often on the 15th and the last day of the month. This is called semi-monthly pay.

What Gets Deducted From Your Salary

Four deductions come out of most payslips.

SSS contribution

SSS contributions are based on your Monthly Salary Credit (MSC), which is your salary placed into a bracket. Your share is 5% of the MSC. Your employer pays a separate, larger share on top of that. The MSC goes up to ₱35,000, so the most an employee pays is ₱1,750 a month. You can find your bracket in our SSS Contribution Table.

PhilHealth premium

The PhilHealth premium is 5% of your monthly basic salary, split equally between you and your employer. Your share is 2.5%. Salaries below ₱10,000 are treated as ₱10,000, and salaries above ₱100,000 are treated as ₱100,000. Our PhilHealth Contribution Calculator does this for you.

Pag-IBIG contribution

Employees earning more than ₱1,500 a month contribute 2% of their pay. The contribution is capped because the maximum fund salary is ₱10,000. So the most you pay is ₱200 a month, even if you earn much more.

Withholding tax

Income tax is based on your taxable income for the year. Under the TRAIN Law, the first ₱250,000 a year is not taxed. Employees who earn the minimum wage are exempt from income tax on that wage. Tax is withheld from each paycheck, and your employer pays it to the BIR.

For the laws and agencies behind these rules, see our Sources page.

How to Compute Take-Home Pay, Step by Step

Step 1: Start with your gross pay

Add your basic salary and any taxable allowances. This is your gross monthly pay.

Step 2: Work out your SSS, PhilHealth and Pag-IBIG shares

Use the rules above. Only your own share comes out of your pay. Your employer's share is separate.

Step 3: Find your taxable income

Subtract the three contributions from your gross pay. These contributions are not taxed, so they lower your taxable income.

Step 4: Work out the withholding tax

Multiply the monthly taxable income by 12 to get a yearly figure. Then apply the tax table below and divide the result by 12.

Annual income tax table under the TRAIN Law
Annual taxable incomeTax
Up to ₱250,000No tax
Over ₱250,000 to ₱400,00015% of the amount over ₱250,000

Step 5: Subtract everything from your gross pay

What is left is your take-home pay.

Worked Example: ₱25,000 Monthly Salary

Say you earn a basic salary of ₱25,000 a month, with no allowances.

The tax part works like this: ₱275,100 is ₱25,100 above ₱250,000. At 15%, that is ₱3,765 a year, or ₱313.75 a month.

Why Your Payslip May Be Different

Your payslip and the calculator can show different numbers. Common reasons:

  • Voluntary deductions, such as Pag-IBIG MP2 savings
  • Company loans, SSS loans or Pag-IBIG loans
  • Absences, late arrivals, undertime or unpaid leave
  • Tax-exempt allowances, such as de minimis benefits
  • Your employer's own payroll rules and rounding
  • A year-end tax adjustment

There is one more reason. The calculator multiplies your monthly taxable income by 12 to work out the tax. Your employer may use the BIR withholding table or adjust the tax at year end, so a small difference in tax is normal. If you enter allowances in the calculator, enter only the taxable part.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

What is the difference between gross pay and take-home pay? ▾

Gross pay is your total earnings before deductions. Take-home pay is what is left after SSS, PhilHealth, Pag-IBIG and withholding tax.

Are minimum wage earners exempt from income tax? ▾

Yes, on their statutory minimum wage. They still pay SSS, PhilHealth and Pag-IBIG contributions.

Does my employer's share come out of my salary? ▾

No. Your employer pays its own share separately. Only your share is deducted from your pay.

Is 13th month pay included in take-home pay? ▾

Not in your regular monthly figure. The 13th month pay is usually paid separately. Under the TRAIN Law, up to ₱90,000 of 13th month pay and other benefits is exempt from income tax.

Why is my payslip different from the calculator? ▾

Loans, absences, voluntary deductions and your employer's payroll rules are the usual causes. Your employer may also use the BIR withholding table or adjust the tax at year end, so a small difference in tax is normal.
